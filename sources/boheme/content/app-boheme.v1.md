# STORY (shown on the opera's page)
Paris, the Latin Quarter. Four penniless friends share a freezing garret: Rodolfo the poet, Marcello the painter, Schaunard the musician and Colline the philosopher. On Christmas Eve Rodolfo stays behind to finish an article, and his neighbour Mimì knocks to ask for a light; within the hour they are in love. At the Café Momus that night Marcello's old flame Musetta, now kept by the elderly Alcindoro, wins Marcello back and leaves Alcindoro to pay the bill. By February Rodolfo's jealousy hides a worse fear: Mimì is gravely ill and he is too poor to care for her. They agree to part when the spring comes. Months later, back in the garret, Musetta brings Mimì in, dying; she wanted to be with Rodolfo at the end, and she dies there among the friends.

## Act 1
where: A garret in the Latin Quarter, Christmas Eve
- Rodolfo and Marcello are too cold to work: Marcello's hands are numb over his painting of the Red Sea, and the stove is empty. Rodolfo feeds it his play, one act at a time, while the philosopher Colline, back from a pawnshop shut for the holiday, joins them at the fire. Then two shop boys arrive with wood, food, wine and cigars, and Schaunard the musician throws down silver coins: an Englishman hired him to play until a parrot died, and he hurried things along with a sprig of parsley. Nobody listens to the story. Schaunard locks the food away for leaner days: on Christmas Eve they will drink at home and dine out.
- The landlord Benoit knocks for the rent. The friends pour him wine, get him boasting of a flirtation at the Mabille dance hall, and then throw him out in mock outrage at a married man's morals. The others leave for the Café Momus; Rodolfo stays five minutes to finish an article.
- Their neighbour Mimì knocks: her candle has gone out on the stairs. She nearly faints, recovers, leaves, and comes back for the key she has dropped; both candles blow out, and in the dark Rodolfo finds the key and pockets it. Their hands meet. He tells her he is a poet (“Che gelida manina”); she tells him she embroiders silk flowers and lives alone under the roofs (“Sì, mi chiamano Mimì”). The friends shout up from the courtyard; in the moonlight Rodolfo and Mimì declare their love (“O soave fanciulla”) and go out arm in arm to join them.

## Act 2
where: The Latin Quarter and the Café Momus, the same evening
- The square is packed with shoppers, street sellers and children. Schaunard buys a horn and a pipe, Colline has his old coat mended and finds a rare book, and Rodolfo buys Mimì a pink bonnet. At a table outside the Café Momus Rodolfo introduces her to his friends; the toy seller Parpignol passes with a cart of toys and a crowd of children behind him, and the friends order supper.
- Musetta arrives with Alcindoro, a rich, fussy old man, and does all she can to make Marcello look at her, until she sings a waltz straight at him about the effect she has on men (“Quando me'n vo'”). Pretending her shoe pinches, she sends Alcindoro off to buy her another pair and falls into Marcello's arms.
- The bill comes and the friends' pockets are empty. As a military tattoo marches through the square Musetta has the two bills put together for the absent Alcindoro to pay; Marcello and Colline carry her, one shoe off, behind the band, and Alcindoro comes back with the new shoes to find only the bill.

## Act 3
where: A toll gate on the edge of Paris, a snowy dawn at the end of February
- Customs men let in street sweepers, milk women and farm women; from the tavern by the gate come drinking songs and Musetta's voice. Mimì arrives coughing and sends for Marcello, who now lives at the tavern with Musetta, painting signs and giving lessons for his keep. She cannot go in: Rodolfo, asleep inside, is so jealous that they cannot go on together, and she begs Marcello to help them part.
- Rodolfo comes out, and she hides. He tells Marcello he wants to leave Mimì because she is a flirt; pressed, he admits the truth: she is gravely ill, his cold room is making her worse, and love is not enough to save her. Her coughing gives her away. Marcello runs back inside when he hears Musetta laughing with a customer.
- Mimì says goodbye without bitterness and asks Rodolfo to send back her few things, but to keep the bonnet if he likes (“Donde lieta uscì”). Remembering what they will lose, they decide to stay together until the spring, while Marcello and Musetta come out quarrelling, trade insults and part.

## Act 4
where: The garret, some time later
- Rodolfo and Marcello pretend to work, each claiming not to care that he has seen his old love riding in a carriage, then give up and think of them (“O Mimì, tu più non torni”). Schaunard and Colline bring bread and a herring, and the four turn supper into a mock banquet, a dance and a duel with the fire irons.
- Musetta bursts in: Mimì is on the stairs, too weak to climb them. She has left her rich viscount and wanted to die near Rodolfo. There is nothing in the house; Musetta gives Marcello her earrings to sell for medicine and a doctor, and goes with him to buy Mimì a muff for her cold hands. Colline says goodbye to his old coat before taking it to the pawnshop (“Vecchia zimarra”), and takes Schaunard with him to leave the lovers alone.
- Mimì and Rodolfo remember the night they met (“Sono andati?”). The others come back; Mimì takes the muff, thinking it is from Rodolfo, and drifts off to sleep while Musetta prays. Schaunard sees that she has died; Rodolfo is the last to understand.

# CAST (name, voice type, one-line description)
The score prints no list of characters (the first page is the opening of Quadro Primo), so the voice types below come from the clefs and ranges in this score and from standard practice.
- [C-rodolfo] Rodolfo (Tenor): A poet, who shares the garret with his three friends.
- [C-mimi] Mimì (Soprano): Rodolfo's neighbour, who embroiders flowers on linen and silk for a living; her real name is Lucia.
- [C-marcello] Marcello (Baritone): A painter, Rodolfo's closest friend, still in love with Musetta.
- [C-musetta] Musetta (Soprano): Marcello's old flame, a flirt who lives off rich admirers but always comes back to him.
- [C-schaunard] Schaunard (Baritone): A musician, the friend who comes home with money.
- [C-colline] Colline (Bass): A philosopher, the friend with a coat full of books.
- [C-benoit] Benoit (Bass): The friends' landlord, who comes for the rent. (Sometimes sung by a tenor; often doubled with Alcindoro.)
- [C-alcindoro] Alcindoro (Bass): A rich, elderly admirer who pays for Musetta's dresses. (Sometimes sung by a tenor; often doubled with Benoit.)
- [C-parpignol] Parpignol (Tenor): A toy seller who wheels his cart through the Latin Quarter on Christmas Eve.
- [C-sergente] Sergente (Bass): The sergeant of the customs post at the toll gate.
- [C-doganiere] Doganiere (Bass): A customs officer at the toll gate.

Small solo lines (not app characters): a street seller crossing the square in Act 2 (“Un venditore ambulante”, tenor: “Prugne di Tours!”); a boy in the children's chorus in Act 2 (“Ragazzo solo”: “Vo' la tromba, il cavallin!”).

Chorus groups (not app characters):
- Act 2: the street sellers (Venditori); the crowd of students, working girls, townsfolk and shopkeepers (La folla: Studenti, Sartine, Borghesi, Bottegaie); some women sellers (Alcune venditrici); urchins (Monelli); customers calling from the café (Dal Caffè); the children round Parpignol's cart (Bambine e ragazzi) and their mothers (Le mamme).
- Act 3: the street sweepers at the gate (Spazzaturai); women and men singing inside the tavern; the milk women (Lattivendole); the carters (Carrettieri); the farm women (Paesane).

    Rodolfo, reassured that she is sleeping, asks quietly about the doctor. Musetta warms the medicine and prays to the Madonna to spare Mimì, who is an angel. Schaunard goes to the bed and whispers to Marcello that she has died; Colline comes back with the money from his coat. Rodolfo hangs Musetta's cape over the window to keep the sun off Mimì's face, then sees how the others are looking at him. Marcello embraces him (‘Coraggio’), and Rodolfo throws himself on Mimì's body, crying her name.

# CHARACTER ARCS (act by act)
[A-rodolfo-1] Rodolfo · Act 1: Freezing in the garret, he burns his own play for warmth, joins the feast Schaunard's money buys and the teasing of the landlord, then stays behind to write. His neighbour Mimì knocks for a light; he pockets her lost key so as to go on searching with her in the dark, tells her who he is (“Che gelida manina”), and they fall in love in the moonlight (“O soave fanciulla”).
[A-rodolfo-2] Rodolfo · Act 2: He buys Mimì a pink bonnet in the Christmas crowd, is a little jealous when she looks at the students, and presents her proudly to his friends at the Café Momus. Watching Musetta's antics, he tells Mimì he would never forgive such behaviour, and at the end he marches off with her behind the band.
[A-rodolfo-3] Rodolfo · Act 3: After walking out on Mimì in the night, he tells Marcello at the toll gate that he wants to leave her because she is a flirt; pressed, he admits the truth: she is dying, and his poverty is killing her. Discovering that she has heard, he tries to take it back; she says goodbye, and they agree to stay together until the spring.
[A-rodolfo-4] Rodolfo · Act 4: Alone again, he pretends not to care that Mimì has a rich protector, then broods over the bonnet she left (“O Mimì, tu più non torni”). When Musetta brings Mimì back dying, he lays her on his bed; alone with her he gives back the bonnet and remembers how they met (“Sono andati?”). He is the last to realise that she has died.
[A-mimi-1] Mimì · Act 1: Her candle out on the stairs, she knocks at her neighbour's door, nearly faints, and comes back for the key she has dropped. In the dark the poet takes her hand; she tells him she embroiders flowers and lives alone under the roofs (“Sì, mi chiamano Mimì”), and in the moonlight she falls in love (“O soave fanciulla”) and goes out with him to meet his friends.
[A-mimi-2] Mimì · Act 2: Rodolfo buys her a pink bonnet and introduces her to his friends at the Café Momus. She sees at once that Musetta is still in love with Marcello and pities her, and tells Rodolfo she loves him and is all his.
[A-mimi-3] Mimì · Act 3: Coughing, she finds Marcello at the toll gate and begs him to help her and Rodolfo part, because his jealousy makes their life impossible. Hidden, she hears Rodolfo confess that she is dying. She says goodbye without bitterness, telling him to keep the bonnet (“Donde lieta uscì”), but they decide to stay together until the spring.
[A-mimi-4] Mimì · Act 4: Dying, she has left her rich viscount; Musetta finds her in the street and, at her wish, brings her back to Rodolfo's garret. Left alone with him, she remembers their first night (“Sono andati?”), takes Musetta's muff believing it is Rodolfo's gift, and falls asleep for the last time.
[A-marcello-1] Marcello · Act 1: The painter is too cold to paint his Red Sea, jokes about Musetta's icy heart, leads the teasing of the landlord, and goes ahead to the Café Momus, calling back up that Rodolfo has found poetry.
[A-marcello-2] Marcello · Act 2: Bitter about love, he sees Musetta arrive with an old admirer and pretends not to care, describing her to Mimì as a bird of prey that feeds on hearts. Her waltz (“Quando me'n vo'”) is aimed at him and he cannot resist; once she has sent the old man away he takes her in his arms and carries her off behind the band.
[A-marcello-3] Marcello · Act 3: Now living at the tavern by the toll gate with Musetta, he hears Mimì's despair over Rodolfo's jealousy, then Rodolfo's confession that she is dying. Catching Musetta flirting with a customer, he quarrels with her and they part with insults.
[A-marcello-4] Marcello · Act 4: Missing Musetta, he cannot paint anything but her face (“O Mimì, tu più non torni”), then fools about with the others until Musetta brings Mimì in. He sells Musetta's earrings for medicine and fetches the doctor, and is the one who embraces Rodolfo when Mimì has died.
[A-musetta-1] Musetta · Act 2: She arrives at the Café Momus with her elderly admirer Alcindoro, makes a scene to catch Marcello's eye, and sings him her waltz (“Quando me'n vo'”). Pretending her shoe hurts, she sends Alcindoro off for a new pair, falls into Marcello's arms, leaves Alcindoro both bills, and is carried off behind the band.
[A-musetta-2] Musetta · Act 3: Her voice is heard from the tavern at dawn, teaching the customers a drinking song. Caught flirting by Marcello, she insists on her freedom and storms off after a shouting match.
[A-musetta-3] Musetta · Act 4: Finding Mimì dying in the street, she brings her back to Rodolfo's garret, gives Marcello her earrings to sell for medicine, and buys Mimì the muff she longs for. While the medicine warms she prays to the Madonna to spare Mimì.
[A-schaunard-1] Schaunard · Act 1: He comes home in triumph with money, food and firewood, earned from an English lord whose parrot he poisoned with parsley, and decides the friends will drink at home and dine out. He helps empty the landlord's glass, then leads the way to the Café Momus.
[A-schaunard-2] Schaunard · Act 2: He buys a horn and a pipe in the Christmas crowd, orders supper in style because there is a lady at the table, and enjoys the comedy of Musetta and Marcello, until his money turns out to be gone. He marches off behind the band blowing his horn.
[A-schaunard-3] Schaunard · Act 4: He brings home four rolls for a mock banquet and fights a mock duel with Colline. When Mimì is brought in, he sees she has not long to live, leaves the lovers alone, and is the first to see that she has died.
[A-colline-1] Colline · Act 1: The philosopher comes in frozen from a pawnshop shut for Christmas Eve, warms himself at the fire of Rodolfo's play, joins in throwing out the landlord, and is told to get a shave before going out. He falls down the dark stairs on the way.
[A-colline-2] Colline · Act 2: He has his old coat mended, stuffs its pockets with books, and finds a rare Runic grammar. At the Café Momus he admits Mimì to the company in mock Latin and watches Musetta's performance, preferring his pipe and a Greek text to any woman. At the end he helps carry Musetta off behind the band.
[A-colline-3] Colline · Act 4: He brings a herring to the mock banquet and duels with Schaunard using the fire tongs. When Mimì is brought in dying, he takes off his old coat to pawn it for her (“Vecchia zimarra”), and comes back with the money too late.
[A-benoit-1] Benoit · Act 1: The landlord comes for the rent, accepts a glass or two, and is flattered into boasting of his flirtations; then the tenants pretend to be shocked at a married man's morals and throw him out without paying.
[A-alcindoro-1] Alcindoro · Act 2: Musetta's elderly admirer trails after her through the crowd, laden and out of breath, and is embarrassed by her scenes at the Café Momus. Sent running for a new pair of shoes, he comes back to find her gone and the friends' bill waiting for him, and collapses into a chair.
[A-parpignol-1] Parpignol · Act 2: The toy seller's cries are heard in the distance, then he wheels his cart of toys into the square with the children dancing after him, and leads them away again.
[A-sergente-1] Sergente · Act 3: At dawn the sergeant has the toll gate opened for the day's traffic, shows Mimì the tavern where the painter works, and waves the travellers through.
[A-doganiere-1] Doganiere · Act 3: Woken by the street sweepers banging on the gate, the customs officer lets them in, announces the milk women, and checks a basket for duty: empty.

# OPEN QUESTIONS
1. No cast list in the score. The PDF starts on p. 1 with the music of Quadro Primo, so there is no list of characters or voices. Voice types come from the clefs and ranges in the print and from standard practice. Benoit, Alcindoro, the Sergente and the Doganiere are all written in the bass clef; Parpignol and the venditore ambulante use the treble clef with the octave sign, like Rodolfo. Castel's list gives Benoit and Alcindoro as “bass (or tenor)”.
2. Benoit's name. The print spells it without a circumflex: “BENOIT” on p. 33 and “Beno-it!” in the words. The draft follows the print ("Benoit"); the brief wrote “Benoît”. Which should the app use?
3. “Si. Mi chiamano Mimì” (p. 69, sys 3). The print has “Si.” with no grave accent (checked at high zoom); the word is “Sì.” elsewhere. The title follows the print, but the cast-page synopsis quotes it the usual way (“Sì, mi chiamano Mimì”). Keep the print's spelling, or correct it as a misprint?
4. “Che gelida manina” (p. 64): Mimì's startled “Ah!” falls in the first bar of (30), so she counts as singing in the section. The title leaves it out rather than reading “Ah!… Che gelida manina”.
5. Where S1 meets S2 (p. 20): the final “-tor!” of “Abbasso l'autor!” sits in the bar marked (10) ALLEGRO, so S2 starts in the next bar, with the orchestra's “f deciso” entry as the shop boys come in.
6. Start of S3 (p. 33): Benoit's “Beno-it!” crosses the double bar at (17) ALL.º VIVO, so the section starts two bars earlier, at the change to 2/4 that leads into his knocks.
7. S5 “Non sono in vena.” starts at (25) on p. 54, where Rodolfo is alone trying to write, rather than at the LENTO (“Chi è là!?”) four bars before Mimì speaks. Both joints are clean; I chose the earlier one so that his solitude belongs to the scene.
8. S8 “Ehi! Rodolfo!” (pp. 75–77) runs only about a minute. The other choice is to fold it into the duet as “Ehi! Rodolfo!… O soave fanciulla”, but the print's LARGO SOSTENUTO at (41) is a clear start for the duet.
9. Musetta's arrival in Act 2 has no section of its own. Marcello's “Ch'io beva del | tossico!” crosses the double bar at (16) on pp. 115–116, so S12 runs from (15) on p. 111 to p. 129, about 5 minutes. For the same reason Rodolfo's presentation of Mimì (“Questa è Mi-|mì”, p. 103, (10)) is not split from S11.
10. S14 “Ahi! qual dolore” (p. 140, sys 2) starts where the waltz breaks off (“strillando, quasi a piacere”, with the piano resting). There is no double bar there; the next double bar (I.º TEMPO, sys 4) would cut off Musetta's cry from the scene it starts.
11. p. 169: the type of “le guardie” is damaged and looks like “ie guardie”. I read it as “le”.
12. Start of S18 “Mimì?!” (p. 179): Marcello's “Mi-|mì?!” crosses the ALLEGRO at (8), so the section starts at his entrance (“esce dal Cabaré”), two bars earlier. The ANDANTE at (9) on p. 181 could not be used either, because his “En-|trate” crosses it.
13. Start of S20 (p. 198, last bar): Rodolfo's upbeat “Mi-” of “Mimì è tanto malata!” sits before the double bar of (21) LENTO TRISTE, so the section starts on that upbeat bar.
14. Start of S21 (p. 206, (26)): Mimì's “Donde lieta uscì” begins in the LENTO MOLTO, three bars after (26), not at the ANDANTINO. Rodolfo's “Vai?” in bar 1 serves as the lead-in. Her “Addio.” and his “Che!” on p. 205 stay at the end of S20.
15. Start of S22 (p. 209, (29)): Mimì's last syllable “-cor” of “senza rancor” falls on the first beat of (29), where Rodolfo begins “Dunque è proprio finita!”. There is no clean bar line; the elision is accepted. His “Addio sogni d'a-|mor” crosses (30) on p. 210, so (30) cannot be used.
16. The quartet's title. The brief calls it “the Act 3 quartet”; its first sung words are Rodolfo's “Dunque è proprio finita!”. The quartet proper (“Addio dolce svegliare alla mattina!”, (30), p. 210) is the same section. Should the title be “Dunque è proprio finita!… Addio dolce svegliare”?
17. “O Mimì tu più non torni” (p. 228) is printed with an opening parenthesis marking an aside, and with no comma after “Mimì”. The title drops the parenthesis and has no comma; the synopsis and arcs, written for readers, use “O Mimì, tu più non torni”. Should these be made consistent?
18. Start of S25 (p. 231, sys 3): Rodolfo's “Che ora sia?” is sung over the duet's last chord, which is tied over from the bar before. The section starts in that bar.
19. Start of S26 (p. 247, sys 3 bar 4): the section starts in the bar of Musetta's entrance, which holds Marcello's upbeat “Mu-”, before the double bar.
20. S27 “Vecchia zimarra” also covers Colline's words to Schaunard and Schaunard's exit (p. 259 sys 4 to p. 261), because Colline's “Schaunard,” is the upbeat to that passage.
21. Small solo lines. Un venditore ambulante (p. 92) and Ragazzo solo (p. 109) are not app characters, following the Figaro model, which leaves out Mozart's “due donne”. Should they become characters?
22. The Doganiere is labelled “UN DOGANIERE” on p. 170 and “DOGANIERE” on pp. 173 and 178. I have treated these as one customs officer.
23. Musetta in Act 3, S16, sings offstage from the tavern. She counts as singing and is in her piece list. In S18 Rodolfo is inside the tavern, seen only through the window, so he is not listed as on stage.
24. The lengths are estimates from page counts and tempos, not timings from a recording.
25. Act 4's “where/when”: the print gives only “IN SOFFITTA (Come nel Quadro Primo)”, with no season or time. “Some time later” is my wording. Act 3's late-February date is printed in the set description (p. 167).
