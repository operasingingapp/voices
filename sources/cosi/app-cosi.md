# STORY (shown on the opera's page)
Naples, in the course of one day. Two officers, Ferrando and Guglielmo, are certain that their fiancées, the sisters Dorabella and Fiordiligi, ladies from Ferrara, will always be faithful. Their old friend Don Alfonso, a philosopher, bets them a hundred zecchini that the sisters are like all other women, on condition that the two say nothing to them and do whatever he tells them. The officers pretend to leave for the war and come back disguised as two moustached strangers, the 'Albanians', each courting the other's fiancée. The sisters' maid Despina, bribed by Alfonso and apparently not recognising the strangers, helps them: when the strangers pretend to poison themselves for love, she comes in disguised as a doctor and revives them. The sisters hold firm through Act 1. In Act 2 Dorabella gives in to Guglielmo, and Fiordiligi, who holds out longer, to Ferrando. Alfonso tells the furious officers that all women do the same (“così fan tutte”) and has them go through with a double wedding that evening, with Despina disguised as a notary. As soon as the sisters have signed the marriage contract, the officers come back from the 'war' as themselves, find the contract and reveal the trick; Alfonso joins the couples' hands, and all six sing that happy is the man who takes everything the right way.

## Act 1
where: Naples: a coffee house; a garden on the seashore; a room in the sisters' house, with chairs, a small table and three doors; a small garden with two grass banks
- In a coffee house, Ferrando and Guglielmo insist that their fiancées, Dorabella and Fiordiligi, could never betray them (“La mia Dorabella capace non è”). Their old friend Don Alfonso laughs: women's fidelity is like the Arabian phoenix, which everyone says exists and nobody has found (“È la fede delle femmine”). He bets them a hundred zecchini that he can show them, today, that their sweethearts are like all the others; they give their word as soldiers to say nothing to the sisters and to do whatever he tells them, and plan how they will spend the winnings (“Una bella serenata”).
- In a garden by the sea the sisters gaze at their fiancés' portraits and swear to be true (“Ah guarda, sorella”). Alfonso comes in, apparently too upset to speak (“Vorrei dir”): a royal order calls the officers to the battlefield at once. The two say goodbye (“Sento, o Dio, che questo piede”), a chorus sings of the soldier's life (“Bella vita militar”), and the sisters make them swear to write every day (“Di scrivermi ogni giorno”). As the boat moves off, the sisters and Alfonso pray for a gentle wind (“Soave sia il vento”); alone, Alfonso laughs at the two lovers and quotes a proverb: whoever builds his hopes on a woman's heart is ploughing the sea.
- At home, Dorabella gives way to despair (“Smanie implacabili”), and their maid Despina laughs at them: men are never faithful, so women should love for their own convenience (“In uomini, in soldati”). Alfonso bribes Despina with gold to help two rich strangers win her mistresses, and brings in Ferrando and Guglielmo disguised with moustaches; not even Despina recognises them (“Alla bella Despinetta”). Each man courts the other's fiancée. Fiordiligi swears that she will stand firm as a rock against wind and storm (“Come scoglio”), and the sisters storm out while Guglielmo praises the strangers' charms (“Non siate ritrosi”). The officers laugh at Alfonso, sure of winning (“E voi ridete?”), and Ferrando sings that love is all the food he needs (“Un'aura amorosa”).
- In the garden, the rejected strangers burst in, drink what they say is arsenic, and only then see the sisters. Despina, disguised as a doctor, revives them with a magnet; they wake as if delirious and each begs his goddess for a kiss, and the act ends with the sisters refusing in fury.

## Act 2
where: The same day, until the evening: a room in the sisters' house; a garden by the sea, with grass seats, two stone tables and a boat decked with flowers; a room with several doors, a mirror and small tables; a brightly lit hall, with a table laid for four and an orchestra
- Despina tells the sisters to treat love lightly and let the two rich strangers adore them (“Una donna a quindici anni”). Dorabella talks Fiordiligi into a little harmless amusement, and they share out the strangers: Dorabella takes the dark one, Fiordiligi the fair one (“Prenderò quel brunettino”), each choosing, without knowing it, the other's fiancé. In the garden by the sea the 'Albanians' serenade them from a boat decked with flowers (“Secondate, aurette amiche”), and Alfonso and Despina pair the couples off (“La mano a me date”).
- Walking in the garden, Guglielmo gives Dorabella a little heart, takes from her the portrait of Ferrando she wears, and wins her (“Il core vi dono”). Fiordiligi resists Ferrando's pleading (“Ah lo veggio quell'anima bella”), then, alone, begs her absent fiancé to forgive the feeling she is fighting (“Ei parte… Per pietà”). Ferrando reports in triumph that Fiordiligi has sent him away, until Guglielmo shows him the portrait of Ferrando that he took from Dorabella; Guglielmo blames women in general (“Donne mie, la fate a tanti”), and Ferrando, alone, finds that he still loves Dorabella (“Tradito, schernito”).
- Don Alfonso will not pay until he has made one more experiment. Dorabella admits to Despina and her sister that she has given in, and tells Fiordiligi to do the same (“È amore un ladroncello”). Fiordiligi decides instead to put on Ferrando's uniform and join her fiancé at the front, but Ferrando comes in and at last she gives in to him (“Fra gli amplessi in pochi istanti”), while Guglielmo watches. Guglielmo is furious; Alfonso tells the two officers to marry their brides that evening and take them as they are, since all women do it (“Tutti accusan le donne”), and Despina announces that the sisters will marry the 'Albanians'.
- At the wedding supper Fiordiligi, Ferrando and Dorabella drink a toast to forgetting the past while Guglielmo mutters a curse (“E nel tuo, nel mio bicchiero”), and Despina, disguised as a notary, reads the contract; only the sisters sign. The soldiers' chorus is heard again and Alfonso announces that the fiancés have landed. The 'Albanians' hide and slip away, then come back as the officers; they find the contract and the sisters beg for death. The men reveal their disguises, Alfonso confesses his deception and joins the couples' hands, and all six end with the moral (“Fortunato l'uom che prende”).

- [C-alfonso] Don Alfonso (Bass-baritone; the score lists him as Bass): An old philosopher, the two officers' friend.

# CHARACTER ARCS (act by act)
[A-fiordiligi-1] Fiordiligi · Act 1: Sure of her fiancé Guglielmo, she gazes at his portrait with her sister and swears to be true (“Ah guarda, sorella”). When Don Alfonso brings news that the officers are ordered to war, she begs Guglielmo not to go (“Sento, o Dio, che questo piede”), makes him promise to write every day (“Di scrivermi ogni giorno”) and prays for the boat's safe voyage (“Soave sia il vento”). When two moustached strangers declare their love, she orders them out and swears that she will stand firm as a rock (“Temerari, sortite… Come scoglio”). Even when they seem to die of poison for love and are revived by a doctor, she refuses the kiss they beg for, not knowing that the strangers are Guglielmo and Ferrando or that the doctor is Despina.
[A-fiordiligi-2] Fiordiligi · Act 2: Despina and Dorabella talk her into a little harmless amusement, and she takes the fair stranger that Dorabella leaves her, who is Ferrando in disguise (“Prenderò quel brunettino”). She tells him to go and runs from his pleading (“Ah lo veggio quell'anima bella”), but alone she admits that what she feels is no longer virtuous love and begs her absent Guglielmo to forgive her (“Ei parte… Per pietà”). She confesses to her sister and Despina that she loves the fair stranger too, but vows to master herself (“Ora vedo che siete una donna di garbo”). Resolved to die rather than give in, she sends for the officers' uniforms to join her fiancé at the front, but Ferrando finds her and asks for her heart or his death, and she yields (“Fra gli amplessi in pochi istanti”). At the wedding supper she proposes the toast to forgetting the past (“E nel tuo, nel mio bicchiero”) and signs the contract. When the officers come back and the trick is revealed, she blames Don Alfonso and promises to make amends with faith and love.
[A-dorabella-1] Dorabella · Act 1: She shows her sister the fire in the eyes of her fiancé Ferrando's portrait and swears to be true (“Ah guarda, sorella”). When the officers are called to war she cries that Ferrando shall not go (“Sento, o Dio, che questo piede”), asks him to write twice a day (“Di scrivermi ogni giorno”) and prays for a gentle wind (“Soave sia il vento”), and once the boat has left she orders the windows shut and gives way to despair (“Ah scostati… Smanie implacabili”). She calls Despina's mockery of their lovers an insult, orders the moustached strangers out with her sister (“Alla bella Despinetta”), and refuses them a kiss even after their 'poisoning' and cure.
[A-dorabella-2] Dorabella · Act 2: After Despina's lesson, she persuades Fiordiligi that a little amusement is no breach of faith and picks the dark stranger, who is Guglielmo in disguise (“Prenderò quel brunettino”). Walking in the garden she accepts his little heart, and he takes Ferrando's portrait from her and puts the heart in its place (“Il core vi dono”). She tells Despina that she tried to resist, and urges her sister to give in to love as she has (“È amore un ladroncello”). She joins the toast to forgetting the past (“E nel tuo, nel mio bicchiero”), signs the marriage contract, begs for death when the officers come back, and learns at the unmasking that she has been tricked; she promises to make amends with faith and love.
[A-guglielmo-1] Guglielmo · Act 1: Sure that his Fiordiligi cannot betray him (“La mia Dorabella capace non è”), he takes Don Alfonso's bet of a hundred zecchini and plans a banquet with the winnings (“Una bella serenata”). He pretends to leave for the war (“Sento, o Dio, che questo piede”, “Al fato dan legge”, “Di scrivermi ogni giorno”) and comes back in disguise with Ferrando, courting not his own fiancée but Dorabella. When the sisters reject the strangers, he boasts of their charms (“Non siate ritrosi”, or in Mozart's first version “Rivolgete a lui lo sguardo”) and laughs at Alfonso, sure that the bet is won (“E voi ridete?”). He plays the poisoned lover and recognises Despina under the doctor's disguise.
[A-guglielmo-2] Guglielmo · Act 2: He serenades the sisters with Ferrando (“Secondate, aurette amiche”) and quickly wins Dorabella, exchanging a little heart for Ferrando's portrait (“Il core vi dono”), sorry for his friend but bound, he says, by a soldier's honour. He shows Ferrando the portrait, blames women in general (“Donne mie, la fate a tanti”) and, sure of Fiordiligi, claims half the bet. Then he watches her give in to Ferrando (“Fra gli amplessi in pochi istanti”) and rages, but admits that he still loves her and repeats Alfonso's “così fan tutte” (“Tutti accusan le donne”). At the wedding he will not join the toast, and at the unmasking he gives Dorabella back the portrait in exchange for the heart.
[A-ferrando-1] Ferrando · Act 1: Sure that Dorabella is as faithful as she is beautiful (“La mia Dorabella capace non è”), he takes Don Alfonso's bet and plans a serenade with the winnings (“Una bella serenata”). He pretends to leave for the war (“Sento, o Dio, che questo piede”, “Al fato dan legge”, “Di scrivermi ogni giorno”) and comes back in disguise with Guglielmo, courting not his own fiancée but Fiordiligi. Sure of winning after the sisters' angry refusal, he says that love is all the food he needs (“Un'aura amorosa”). He plays the poisoned lover, and on 'waking' calls Fiordiligi his goddess and begs her for a kiss.
[A-ferrando-2] Ferrando · Act 2: He serenades the sisters with Guglielmo (“Secondate, aurette amiche”), but Fiordiligi runs from him and leaves him pining (“Ah lo veggio quell'anima bella”), and he reports her faithfulness to Guglielmo in triumph. When Guglielmo shows him the portrait he took from Dorabella, he wants revenge but finds that he still loves her (“In qual fiero contrasto… Tradito, schernito”). In Alfonso's further test he finds Fiordiligi ready to leave for the front and wins her (“Fra gli amplessi in pochi istanti”), then throws Guglielmo's boast back at him. He joins Alfonso's “così fan tutte” (“Tutti accusan le donne”), goes through the wedding, and at the unmasking bows to Fiordiligi as 'the knight from Albania'.
[A-despina-1] Despina · Act 1: The sisters' maid grumbles about her work, laughs at their despair and tells them that men are never faithful, so women should love for their own convenience (“In uomini, in soldati”). Don Alfonso bribes her with a gold coin and the promise of twenty scudi to help two rich strangers win her mistresses; she does not recognise them as the officers (“Alla bella Despinetta”). She takes charge of the plot, and when the strangers seem to be dying of poison she comes in disguised as a doctor and revives them with a magnet (“Eccovi il medico”).
[A-despina-2] Despina · Act 2: She teaches the sisters how a woman should handle men (“Una donna a quindici anni”), answers for them when the 'Albanians' ask pardon (“La mano a me date”), and tells Fiordiligi she will never master herself. She announces that the sisters will marry the strangers and, disguised as the notary Beccavivi, reads out the marriage contract. Found hiding when the officers come back, she claims she is back from a masked ball; apparently only at the unmasking does she learn that the 'Albanians' were the officers, and that the trick was played on her too, as she has played it on many others.
[A-alfonso-1] Don Alfonso · Act 1: The old philosopher laughs at his friends' faith in women (“È la fede delle femmine”) and bets them a hundred zecchini that their fiancées are no different from other women. He breaks the news of the call to arms as if heartbroken (“Vorrei dir”), laughs aside at the farewells (“Di scrivermi ogni giorno”) and joins the sisters' prayer for a calm sea (“Soave sia il vento”). He bribes Despina, introduces the disguised officers (“Alla bella Despinetta”), tells them to laugh quietly and says aside that their laughter will end in tears (“E voi ridete?”), and stages their 'poisoning' with them.
[A-alfonso-2] Don Alfonso · Act 2: He hurries the sisters to the garden and speaks for the suitors (“La mano a me date”). When Dorabella has given in and Fiordiligi has not, he insists on one more test before he pays, and holds Guglielmo back as Fiordiligi gives in. He tells the two officers to marry their brides anyway, since all women do the same (“Tutti accusan le donne”), arranges the double wedding and brings in the 'notary'. Then he announces the officers' return, drops the signed contract for them to find, and at last admits his deception, joins the couples' hands and tells them all to laugh, as he has.

8. Who knows what. (a) Despina: the libretto never says in so many words when she learns who the 'Albanians' are. Don Alfonso decides to let her into only part of the secret (S25), she asks whether the suitors are rich, and in the Act 2 Finale she is as astonished as the sisters at the unmasking (“Io non so se veglio o sogno”). (b) The sisters: nobody ever tells them about the wager; at the unmasking they learn only that they have been deceived, and they blame Don Alfonso (and, in S72, Despina too).
